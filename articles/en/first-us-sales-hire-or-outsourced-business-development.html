Your first US sales hire or an outsourced partner? A decision guide for lean tech teams

2026-10-21 · Gershon Consulting

For an international technology company, the first US sales decision is often presented as a simple choice: hire a person or appoint a partner.

The more useful starting point is the work that needs to be done.

You may need to identify a credible first buyer segment, adapt the way you explain the product, create visibility, start conversations, book meetings, run technical demonstrations, negotiate terms, or support customers. These tasks belong to the same commercial journey, but they require different capabilities.

A decision becomes easier when you separate those responsibilities and identify which uncertainty matters most today.

This guide is written for founders of international tech businesses with more than $1 million in annual sales and fewer than 20 people. It offers a decision framework, not a promise that one model will outperform the other.

1. Decide whether the first job is discovery or execution

Start with the evidence you already have.

Can you name a specific US buyer segment? Have people in that segment confirmed that the problem matters? Do you know what makes them consider a new supplier? Can you explain the likely buying process and who participates in it?

If these answers remain uncertain, the next commercial investment includes discovery. Someone will need to test the segment, document objections and decide what the responses mean.

If those answers are well supported and you have a repeatable process, the job is closer to execution: cover the market, progress opportunities and improve a process that already has a foundation.

Neither situation is a failure. The problem comes from defining an execution role while expecting the person or partner to discover the market quietly in the background.

Write one sentence that names the immediate job. For example: “Determine whether this buyer segment has a sufficiently urgent problem and an accessible buying process.” Or: “Increase coverage of a segment where our offer and sales process are already established.”

2. Assign the commercial responsibilities

Before comparing providers or candidates, make a responsibility map.

ResponsibilityOwner to name before launch
Buyer-segment definitionFounder or commercial owner, with campaign input
Credible content and proofA named subject expert and an editor/publisher
Prospect selection and initial conversationsThe hire, partner or internal business-development owner
Qualification standardFounder/commercial owner and the person generating conversations
Product demonstrationSomeone with sufficient product and customer context
Proposal, pricing and negotiationThe authorized commercial owner
Follow-up and next-step managementOne named person for each live opportunity
Onboarding and customer supportThe delivery/customer owner
Performance reviewThe founder or accountable commercial leader

The names matter more than the job titles. If a partner books meetings but the founder is unable to attend or follow up, the constraint remains inside the business. If a new employee has no access to product expertise or pricing decisions, hiring alone will not resolve it.

3. When a direct hire may fit

A direct hire can make sense when you have a clearly defined role, sustained work for that role, a credible support structure and a practical way to manage the person.

Look for a situation in which the new colleague can inherit a specific segment, relevant proof and an agreed commercial process. The role may also require sustained ownership of strategic accounts, relationship continuity or close collaboration with product and delivery teams.

Before recruiting, define the reporting line, decision rights, support available, expected travel and how success will be reviewed. Resolve the employment and operating arrangements through appropriate local advisers. Do not leave those questions until after selecting a candidate.

When comparing the investment, include more than headline compensation. Recruiting, onboarding, management time, tools, travel and the time required to learn the offer all belong in your own cost model. Use actual quotations and your circumstances rather than a generic salary figure from a blog post.

4. When an outsourced partner may fit

An outsourced business-development partner can fit a team that needs a defined set of activities and a structured learning process without immediately creating an internal role for all of them.

The scope must be precise. Does the partner research buyers, write content, run outreach, qualify responses, book meetings, prepare briefings, or support account management? Who owns the records and can export them? Who can approve content and change targeting? How are disagreements about fit resolved?

Ask for definitions and operating examples. A report containing many activities is less useful than a clear account of which companies engaged, what they said and what happened next.

Your internal obligations remain important. Provide product context, approved proof and timely feedback. Assign someone to handle the conversations that require your expertise or commercial authority. Confirm the service's actual boundaries before assuming that business development includes every stage through contract signature.

5. Consider a hybrid model deliberately

A hybrid model can combine founder-led selling or an internal salesperson with external support for visibility, prospect research, conversations and meeting preparation.

It works best when the handoff is clear. Define when a response becomes an opportunity, what information accompanies it, who accepts ownership and what feedback returns to the team generating the conversation.

Avoid splitting ownership so finely that nobody is accountable for the next step. Every live opportunity should have one owner and one agreed action.

You may also sequence the models. A bounded business-development phase can produce evidence for a future hiring decision. An existing internal seller may use a partner to improve coverage of a specific segment. The correct sequence depends on your current bottleneck and available management capacity.

6. Compare the models with the same decision sheet

Use the same questions for a candidate, partner and hybrid arrangement:

  • What exact problem does this option address?
  • Which buyer segment will it cover first?
  • Which activities and outcomes are within scope?
  • What work remains with the founder and product team?
  • What information and records will the company own?
  • What is the complete resource commitment, including internal time?
  • What evidence would justify continuing?
  • What would trigger a change in segment, offer or operating model?
  • What is the orderly exit or transition process?

Agree the evaluation period around the work involved and your actual sales cycle. A technical sale with several stakeholders may not mature on the same schedule as a small, familiar purchase. Do not confuse early activity, a first opportunity and closed revenue.

7. Define useful progress

Track the journey in stages: relevant company, response, verified fit, confirmed meeting, meeting held, proposal and client.

Keep the denominator and date visible. “Four meetings” is incomplete without knowing whether those meetings were booked, attended and relevant to the segment. A new inquiry and a deal acquired months earlier should not be combined to invent a current close rate.

Record why conversations stop. Repeated objections can reveal an unclear offer, an unsuitable segment, a missing proof point or an internal follow-up problem. Those findings may change the next decision more than a larger activity count would.

For a small team, a short review with clear records is preferable to an elaborate dashboard that nobody uses to act.

The decision to make now

Choose the operating model that addresses your current constraint and leaves responsibility clear.

If the first task is learning, define the question and the evidence required. If the first task is execution, define the role and support it needs. If the work spans both, make the handoffs explicit.
